# Teaching with Market Lab

A ready-to-run lesson, the discussion questions that make it land, and a marking
rubric. Everything here works with no accounts, no logins and no internet.

Set-up for the teacher is one line: **send students the link, tell them the
seed.** That is the whole thing.

---

## Before the lesson

1. Open the app yourself and play one ten-year run. Ten minutes.
2. Pick a seed and write it on the board. Each run length has its own default —
   `classroom-4019` for ten years — and all three are good teaching markets. See
   *What happens in the default seeds* below.
3. Decide whether you want **Solo mode** (everyone against the market) or **Comp
   mode** (everyone races the same AI). Comp mode is better for a first lesson;
   there is an opponent to beat and it is more fun.

Students need: a device, the link, and the seed spelled exactly. **The seed is
case-sensitive** — `Classroom-4019` is a completely different market.

---

## A 50-minute lesson

### 1. Predict (5 min, before anyone opens the app)

Write the sixteen assets on the board. Ask every student to write down, on
paper:

- Which one do you think will do best over ten years?
- Which will do worst?
- Where would you put $1,000 today?

Collect these. They are the assessment evidence, and the reveal at the end is
the whole lesson.

### 2. Set up (5 min)

Everyone opens the app. A four-card briefing comes up on a first visit —
**let them read it**, it covers where the money comes from, what moves prices,
and the fees. It can be reopened any time from **How it works** in the header.

Then they enter the seed, pick a goal, and press **Start over**. Check that the
seed matches around the room — one typo and that student is playing a different
decade.

Worth saying out loud, because it is the question you will get most: the balance
climbs every month even if a student buys nothing, because $50 keeps arriving.
That is their own money, not profit. The briefing says so, but say it again.

### 3. Play (20 min)

Students buy what they want and press **Run the simulation**. It advances on its
own and stops only for crashes, where it asks what to do.

While it runs, walk the room and ask:

- What phase is the economy in? What does that mean for your shares?
- What does your goal bar say you need per year now?
- How much have you paid in fees so far? How many trades was that?

### 4. The reveal (10 min)

Everyone finishes and reads out their **result code**. Paste them into the
**Class leaderboard** and rank the class on the board.

Then put the predictions back on the desk and compare.

### 5. Discuss (10 min)

Use the questions below. This part matters more than the game.

---

## Discussion questions

**On chasing winners**

- Nvidia returned about 33% a year for twenty years. The app expects about 4%
  from it. Why would anyone set it that low?
- Who put money into the thing that had gone up the most recently? How did that
  go?
- If a company is obviously excellent, why doesn't that make it a good buy?

**On risk**

- Ethereum moves about six times as much as a world fund. Does it earn six times
  as much? What does it actually earn?
- Look at the worst fall in your results. Would you have sold at that point if it
  had been real money?
- Two people finish with the same amount, one dipped 10% and one fell 55%. Are
  those the same investment?

**On fees**

- How much did you pay in fees? What fraction of what you put in was that?
- Why is a $3 fee brutal on a $50 trade and irrelevant on a $5,000 one?
- Who traded the most? Who traded the least? Who finished ahead?

**On the economy** *(the part that connects to the rest of your course)*

- What happened to your shares when the economy went into recession?
- Why do government bonds go *up* when shares go down?
- The app makes correlations rise during a recession — different things start
  falling together. Why does that make diversification less useful exactly when
  you need it most?
- About 63% of months here are expansion and 11% recession. What does that
  suggest about trying to time when to be in the market?

**On the real world**

- The app assumes nobody can predict returns. Is that fair? What would it mean
  if it were wrong?
- Your KiwiSaver will be invested for about 45 years. Based on this, what fund
  would you pick at your first job?

## What happens in the default seeds

Do not tell students beforehand. Changing the run length swaps the default seed
with it, because a market picked to be fair over ten years is not fair over
five or twenty.

| | 5 years<br>`classroom-3756` | 10 years<br>`classroom-4019` | 20 years<br>`classroom-3094` |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total World Fund | +7.4% a year | **+7.9%** a year | **+8.5%** a year |
| S&P/NZX 50 Fund | +2.5% a year | +8.6% a year | +8.4% a year |
| NZ Government Bonds | +4.1% a year | +3.8% a year | +3.7% a year |
| Bitcoin | **−32.3%** a year | **−9.6%** a year | +2.1% a year |
| Best on the board | KiwiSaver fund, +9.7% | Fisher & Paykel, +9.2% | S&P 500 fund, +8.5% |
| Worst on the board | Bitcoin, −32.3% | Mainfreight, −10.5% | Tesla, −10.0% |
| Crash decisions | 3 | 3 | 6 |

All three pass through every phase of the business cycle and all three have a
fall of at least a quarter to sit through.

The teaching point is the same in each: **the boring diversified fund beat
almost everything**, and the best single company only just edged out the fund
while carrying several times the risk. Nobody was rewarded for excitement.

The twenty-year run makes an extra point worth drawing out. Bitcoin *did* make
money — +2.1% a year — and still finished behind bonds and miles behind the
world fund. "It went up" and "it was a good investment" are not the same claim.

Run it a second time with a different seed to make the other half of the point:
a single decade is one roll of the dice.

## Marking rubric

Suitable for a short written reflection after the lesson.

| | Not yet | Achieved | Merit | Excellence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Explains their strategy** | States what they bought | Explains why they bought it | Links the choice to risk and return | Evaluates whether the reasoning was sound independently of how it turned out |
| **Uses evidence from the run** | No figures | Quotes their final value | Uses several figures — fees, worst fall, growth per year | Compares their run against the benchmark and the class, and interprets the difference |
| **Understands risk** | Treats high return as simply good | States that risk and return are related | Explains that risk widens the range of outcomes | Distinguishes expected return from a typical outcome, and explains volatility drag |
| **Understands the cycle** | Does not mention it | Names the phase they were in | Links a phase to what happened to prices | Explains why correlations rise in a downturn and what that means for diversifying |
| **Reflects honestly** | Attributes result entirely to luck or skill | Recognises both played a part | Identifies a specific decision that helped or hurt | Distinguishes a good decision from a good outcome, using the same-seed comparison as evidence |

**The Excellence line worth watching for:** a student who lost money but can
explain that their decisions were sound and the market was unkind has understood
more than one who won and thinks it was skill.

## Extension activities

- **Same seed, different strategies.** Half the class buys and holds one world
  fund; half trades every month. Compare the fees tile.
- **Same strategy, different seeds.** Everyone buys the world fund and holds, but
  each uses a different seed. The spread of results is entirely luck — a good
  demonstration of why one year of a fund's performance tells you almost nothing.
- **Beat the Patient AI.** In Comp mode on Hard, the opponent buys a world fund,
  adds to it yearly, and buys more during crashes. It trades about 13 times in a
  decade and wins about 70% of the time. Ask students to design a strategy that
  beats it, then test it.
- **Twenty years.** Run the same seed at 20 years and watch the range of outcomes
  narrow. Ethereum's 10th-percentile return improves from −23.5% a year over ten
  years to −14.8% over twenty.

## A word of caution worth passing on

There is a fair criticism of stock market games in general: by compressing years
into minutes and rewarding whoever finishes highest, they can make investing feel
like gambling, and teach exactly the wrong habit.

This app is built against that, deliberately:

- Chasing recent winners **loses** here, and the code is arranged so it loses for
  honest reasons — fees and mistiming, not a rigged dice roll.
- The hardest AI opponent is the one that does the least.
- The end screen prices your panic-selling in dollars.
- Every asset shows a past return next to a much lower expected one.

But the leaderboard still ranks people by who finished highest, and one ten-year
run is one roll of the dice. If a student wins by putting everything into
crypto, say so out loud: they made a bad decision and got a good outcome, and the
two are not the same thing. Running the same strategy on a second seed usually
makes the point for you.

## Checking the numbers before you teach from them

The historical return figures shown next to each company are **approximate and
rounded**, included as teaching context rather than data. Only the fund-level NZX
50 figure has been checked against a source. The per-company numbers are
estimates and are sensitive to the start date chosen.

None of it affects the simulation, which never reads those figures — but they are
on screen next to real company names, so check them against a current source
before teaching from them, and edit `js/market.js` if you want to correct them.
